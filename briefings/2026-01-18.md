# Liquidity Reallocation in the U.S. Dollar System — Part II: Deposit Mobility and Bank Funding Substitution

## Weekend Deep Dive

*January 18, 2026*

Good morning.

**TL;DR**  
The U.S. banking system has looked calm in aggregate even as its funding has become more fragile in distribution. Higher rates, faster transfer rails, and altered risk perception have turned deposits from a passive base into a managed liability. Stablecoins add one new exit door, but the larger story is a broad repricing and reshuffling of cash. Banks are adapting through higher deposit costs, greater use of brokered and wholesale funding, and heavier liquidity buffers, all under regulatory constraints that shape behavior as much as they protect the system.

This is the second of a three-part series examining stablecoins, deposit behavior, and bank funding as interlinked features of modern dollar liquidity. The first piece examined stablecoins as narrow, reserve-backed instruments. This piece focuses on how deposit behavior and bank funding respond to increased mobility. The final piece examines where this mobile money goes at the system level, and when reallocation becomes liquidity stress.

---

### Scope

This deep dive examines deposit flight and bank funding pressure, with stablecoins treated as one structural competitor among several. The emphasis is on how pressure shows up inside balance sheets and funding mixes, rather than on predicting outcomes.

---

### Calm on the surface, fragile underneath

At the system level, deposits have not vanished. Aggregate balances remain large, and the banking system continues to fund itself day to day. Federal Reserve H.8 data make this clear.

What has changed is not the level of deposits, but their behavior. The mix has shifted away from non-interest transaction accounts toward time deposits, brokered balances, and wholesale substitutes. Money did not leave the system; it moved, repriced, and became more sensitive to confidence and yield.

A system can look stable in aggregate while becoming fragile in distribution. Stress does not require a generalized run. It requires sorting.

---

### Saturday’s map, Sunday’s pressure

The prior deep dive described stablecoins as narrow, reserve-backed liabilities that sit alongside money market funds and Treasury bills. Today’s question is where that structure begins to register inside regulated institutions.

By scale, money market funds and direct Treasury exposure remain far more important drivers of deposit substitution than stablecoins, particularly for institutional cash. Stablecoins add something different: continuous settlement, portability, and global reach. They are not just a yield alternative. They are a mobility alternative.

What emerges is not a single competitor displacing banks, but a widening field of alternatives that collectively erode the assumption that deposits are captive.

---

### The psychology of uninsured deposits

Deposit behavior is less about panic than coordination.

In calm periods, the FDIC insurance cap fades into the background. In stressed periods, it becomes a behavioral dividing line. Uninsured depositors do not need certainty to move; they need a reason not to be last.

A useful metaphor is a crowded theater with one narrow exit. No one stands when the lights flicker. Movement begins when someone smells smoke and realizes there are too many people behind them. At that point, staying seated requires a stronger argument than standing up.

Digital banking has shortened that interval dramatically. Transfers that once took days now take seconds. Coordination happens in group chats rather than branch lobbies. In that environment, deposit stability depends less on fundamentals than on how quickly reassurance can travel relative to fear.

This is why deposit pressure concentrates. Insured retail balances remain comparatively stable. Large corporate, municipal, and institutional balances do not.

---

### SVB, speed, and the liquidity bridge

The Silicon Valley Bank episode in 2023 mattered less for its size than for what it revealed about speed. Deposits left in hours, not days, driven by a highly concentrated uninsured depositor base and near-frictionless transfer rails.

The Bank Term Funding Program changed the second act of that story. By allowing banks to borrow against high-quality securities at par, it reduced the need for forced asset sales and stabilized balance sheets at a critical moment. The facility expanded balance-sheet liquidity at the bank level temporarily. It bought time rather than demand.

Together, the episode and the response clarified two points that continue to shape behavior. Deposit flight in the digital era is fast and selective. Liquidity backstops can prevent fire sales, but they do not restore deposit inertia.

---

### Stablecoins as one more exit door

Stablecoins add a different kind of pressure to this environment.

When dollars move from a bank account into a stablecoin, the bank loses funding immediately. The stablecoin issuer holds reserves that often sit in Treasury bills or repo, outside the deposit-funded lending channel.

Mechanically, this resembles a narrow bank forming in parallel. Functionally, it gives depositors a way to hold dollar-like value without a bank relationship at all.

The effect remains modest in aggregate terms, but meaningful at the margin. Stablecoins compete most directly for balances that value speed, portability, and transparency over relationship and yield labeling.

If deposit substitution were ever to scale to the point of materially constraining bank credit creation, stablecoins would likely shift from being treated as a marginal funding alternative to a policy variable. That shift would not require stablecoins to fail; it would require them to succeed in a way that becomes legible to credit formation and economic growth. In that scenario, the constraint would be political rather than technical.

---

### Institutions follow the yield

Beyond stablecoins, yield has been the dominant force reshaping deposits.

As rates rose, institutional and corporate cash managers reassessed long-standing habits. Money that once sat in operating accounts moved into money market funds, Treasury bills, and direct purchases of short-term government securities.

Banks responded unevenly, reflecting differences in franchise strength, depositor mix, and margin tolerance. Some raised deposit rates and restructured accounts to retain balances. Others accepted outflows in order to preserve margins, relying instead on brokered deposits and wholesale funding.

The consequence was a sharp increase in funding costs for those that chose to compete. More broadly, what changed is not just the price of funding, but its duration: banks are replacing long-assumed, open-ended deposits with funding that reprices faster, rolls more frequently, and demands more active management.

---

### Brokered deposits and wholesale borrowing

When deposits leave, banks replace them. They simply replace them expensively.

Brokered deposits trade speed for cost. They arrive quickly and scale easily, but they carry higher rates and little loyalty. Wholesale borrowing offers reliability, but adds leverage and interest expense.

Federal Home Loan Bank advances proved critical during stress, providing rapid liquidity against pledged collateral. Federal Reserve facilities did as well, even if stigma delayed their use in some cases.

The result is a funding mix that works, but one that is more sensitive to rate changes and confidence shifts. Funding becomes something to manage continuously rather than a quiet foundation. Wholesale funding behaves reliably until it doesn’t, which is another way of saying it behaves exactly as advertised.

---

### Regional banks and uneven burden

These dynamics weigh most heavily on regional and community banks.

Smaller institutions rely more on concentrated depositor bases and local relationships. When those deposits become mobile, the adjustment is sharper. Larger banks benefit from flight-to-safety behavior and diversified funding channels. Smaller banks pay more, hold more liquidity, and accept lower profitability.

Over time, this creates consolidation pressure. The system does not need a policy decision to concentrate. Incentives do that work quietly.

---

### Regulation as guardrail and constraint

Regulatory structures shape this landscape in predictable ways.

Deposit insurance caps influence behavior under stress. Liquidity rules penalize reliance on unstable funding and favor insured, retail-like deposits. Discount-window stigma complicates the use of central-bank backstops even when they are available.

Resolution planning and systemically important bank designations also shape perception. Depositors implicitly rank institutions by survivability, whether or not anyone uses the word bailout.

None of these constraints are errors. They simply mean banks operate in a regime where funding stability is strategic rather than assumed.

---

### What to watch

Several signals tend to reveal whether deposit pressure is easing or intensifying.

Changes in deposit composition matter more than headline totals, particularly shifts between transaction accounts, time deposits, and brokered balances.

Reliance on wholesale funding bears watching, as does the language banks use around contingency funding and liquidity planning.

Relative deposit pricing between large and small banks often signals stress before it appears in reported flows.

At the margin, watch whether stablecoin rails become frictionless enough to make moving out of deposits instantaneous for mainstream users.

Whether deposits regain prior inertia or remain structurally mobile will be revealed through these indicators rather than through headline totals.
