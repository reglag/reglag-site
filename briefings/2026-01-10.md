# When OFAC Writes Checks, It’s Really Writing Signals
## Weekend Deep Dive

This edition covers OFAC civil penalties and published findings of violation in calendar year 2025, compares them with 2024, then draws a strategic line forward. ### How OFAC penalties actually work

OFAC civil penalties are not mechanical fines tied directly to transaction value. They are discretionary instruments shaped by statutory authority, aggravating and mitigating factors, and enforcement priorities. In practice, penalties are used to signal which failures OFAC views as structural rather than incidental.

Historically, enforcement focused on transaction-level violations: exports routed through sanctioned jurisdictions, payments processed for blocked persons, or discrete screening failures. In recent years, enforcement has increasingly emphasized facilitation risk, including ownership structures, governance failures, intermediaries, and platforms that allow sanctioned activity to persist over time.

Understanding that distinction matters. A penalty aimed at facilitation is not primarily about deterrence at the margin. It is about reshaping incentives upstream.

### The analytical year in one sentence

The year was not a huge fines year so much as a normal year plus one enormous message.

In 2025, OFAC published fourteen enforcement actions totaling $265,746,819. The average penalty looks enormous, but the average is not the story. Concentration is.

GVA Capital was assessed $215,988,868. That single action represented about 81% of total penalty dollars, and the three largest actions represented about 90%. Removing GVA leaves a remainder of $49,757,951, which is remarkably close to the $48,790,404 total in 2024.

The size of the year was a deliberate choice, not a broad-based shift in dollar pace. The real shift was where OFAC chose to aim.

### 2024 versus 2025, what actually changed

The distribution became more intentional.

In 2024, twelve actions totaled $48.8 million. Two large industrial and trade cases carried much of the total, but the largest case did not define the year.

In 2025, the same top-heavy structure was turned into a billboard. GVA was not merely the largest case. It was the year’s thesis in dollar form. OFAC does not accidentally create billboards.

The target set shifted from banks to the enabling layer.

In 2025, the most underappreciated change was not how much OFAC fined, but who it fined. Grouped by functional role rather than legal label, the pattern becomes clearer.

Gatekeepers and private capital accounted for three actions totaling approximately $228.6 million, or about 86% of total dollars. Real estate and property services accounted for three actions totaling approximately $12.9 million. Brokerage and market access platforms accounted for one action totaling $11.8 million. Industrial and manufacturing exporters accounted for three actions totaling approximately $6.4 million. Crypto and fintech intermediaries accounted for two actions totaling approximately $3.9 million. Logistics and trade services accounted for two actions totaling approximately $2.2 million.

The story is not that OFAC stopped caring about banks. The story is that OFAC spent the year making examples out of actors that create pathways, hide ownership, and keep sanctioned interests economically viable.

### Every 2025 OFAC case, ordered by dollar value

The list below reflects published 2025 civil penalty actions and findings of violation and is presented to illustrate enforcement concentration and target selection. It is not intended to function as a continuously maintained enforcement tracker.

- **GVA Capital Ltd. — $215,988,868**  
  Venture capital firm knowingly managed investments for a sanctioned Russian oligarch using ownership structures to obscure blocked interests.

- **Interactive Brokers LLC — $11,832,136**  
  Brokerage platform processed transactions for sanctioned persons due to screening and control failures at scale.

- **IPI Partners, LLC — $6,194,789**  
  Private equity firm failed to block and report interests tied to sanctioned Russian persons through energy-infrastructure investments.

- **Family International Realty LLC and owner — $4,768,000**  
  Real estate firm and principal engaged in a scheme to sell property on behalf of a sanctioned Russian national.

- **Gracetown, Inc. — $4,144,651**  
  Property management company failed to block and report interests in real estate owned by a sanctioned person.

- **Unicat Catalyst Technologies, LLC — $3,882,797**  
  Exporter shipped industrial goods through intermediaries that diverted products to sanctioned destinations.

- **Exodus Movement, Inc. — $3,000,000**  
  Digital asset wallet provider allowed transactions from sanctioned jurisdictions due to geolocation and screening failures.

- **Harman International Industries, Inc. — $1,441,651**  
  Manufacturer violated sanctions through overseas subsidiaries and distributors engaging sanctioned markets.

- **Individual fiduciary trust matter — $1,080,000**  
  Trustee administered trust assets for the benefit of a blocked person and failed to treat the property as blocked.

- **Haas Automation, Inc. — $1,049,579**  
  Machine-tool manufacturer exported equipment to sanctioned jurisdictions through third-party distributors.

- **Individual sanctions evasion matter — $565,000**  
  Individual engaged in a scheme to evade Russia-related sanctions through controlled entities.

- **Fracht FWO Inc. — $396,000**  
  Freight forwarder facilitated shipments involving sanctioned jurisdictions through inadequate screening of logistics documentation.

- **ShapeShift AG — $275,000**  
  Crypto exchange facilitated transactions involving sanctioned persons after marketing itself as non-custodial and anonymous.

- **Key Holding, LLC — $153,237**  
  Logistics company routed transactions involving sanctioned persons through third-country intermediaries.

### Mechanisms matter more than sectors

Gatekeeper failures and hidden blocked interests dominated the dollar total. Blocked property and reporting failures defined the real-asset cases. Platform and intermediary control failures characterized brokerage and crypto actions. Trade diversion and third-country routing remained the most persistent operational weakness.

### Working hypothesis

Taken together, the enforcement pattern suggests that OFAC is prioritizing facilitation risk over transactional exposure, using a small number of large penalties to discipline gatekeepers, private capital, and ownership structures that allow sanctioned activity to persist.

If this interpretation holds, future enforcement does not need to be broader to be more effective. It only needs to remain strategically targeted.

### 2026, strategically

If this pattern persists, enforcement is likely to continue emphasizing instructional value over volume. Fewer cases may carry more weight. Facilitation and governance failures may matter more than sector labels. Disclosure and remediation posture may increasingly shape how enforcement unfolds.

OFAC does not need to close every door. It only needs to make the hallways narrow.
