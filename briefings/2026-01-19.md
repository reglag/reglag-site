## Scope

This deep dive focuses on U.S. dollar liquidity plumbing rather than individual firms. It is descriptive and structural. The goal is to give the reader an intuitive map of how liquidity moves once it leaves bank deposits, not to forecast outcomes or advocate policy.

## Liquidity as a system, not a balance

A useful way to think about modern liquidity is as a system with distinct functions: creation, storage, transmission, and absorption.

Banks sit at the center of liquidity creation. When banks extend credit, they create deposits. This process is endogenous and driven by private demand, but it is conditioned by policy rates, regulatory constraints, and risk appetite. Higher policy rates raise the cost of credit creation; capital and liquidity rules shape where and how it occurs.

The central bank is the other source of liquidity creation, but of a different kind. When the Federal Reserve expands its balance sheet, it creates reserves. This is an exogenous and discretionary act, typically used during stress or to implement policy, not as a routine substitute for private credit creation.

Liquidity moves through this system, but it is not fully fungible. Cash, reserves, Treasury bills, repo claims, and deposits are all liquid, but they are not interchangeable across all contexts. Collateral, maturity, legal claims, and counterparties matter for where liquidity can go and how it behaves under stress.

When deposits leave banks, liquidity does not disappear. It is reallocated.

## Where liquidity goes first

In normal conditions, liquidity leaving bank deposits flows into short-dated government instruments.

Treasury bills and government money market funds are the primary destinations. They provide safety, transparency, and immediate liquidity without credit exposure to a single institution. They do not create new liquidity, but they can absorb large volumes quickly.

This reallocation is visible in issuance patterns, fund flows, and changes in deposit composition. It is not inherently destabilizing. It is often a rational response to higher rates and improved access to nonbank instruments.

The key distinction is that these instruments store liquidity; they do not intermediate it into private credit in the same way banks do.

## Transmission depends on balance sheets

Moving liquidity between these destinations requires intermediaries.

Repo markets and dealer balance sheets perform this function. Dealers finance inventories of Treasury securities, connect cash investors with collateral, and allow liquidity to circulate without requiring outright sales.

This transmission capacity is not infinite. Dealer balance sheets are constrained by capital requirements, leverage ratios, and internal risk limits. Repo capacity can tighten precisely when demand for liquidity rises.

When these constraints bind, liquidity continues to move — but it does so under pressure.

## Money market funds are reservoirs with rules

Money market funds play a central role in absorbing liquidity, but they are not frictionless.

They hold liquidity buffers, face redemption dynamics, and operate under regulatory constraints designed to prevent destabilizing runs. Gates, liquidity fees, and portfolio limits shape behavior under stress rather than in calm periods.

When these constraints become binding, private absorption capacity becomes visible, and liquidity seeks alternative holders.

## Stablecoins as accelerants, not absorbers

Stablecoins fit into this system as accelerants.

They do not create new liquidity. They do not expand absorption capacity. Their reserves ultimately sit in the same instruments: Treasury bills, repo, and bank deposits.

What they change is speed and coordination. Stablecoins allow liquidity to move continuously, globally, and with minimal friction. They compress settlement times and synchronize behavior across participants.

They amplify velocity without expanding balance-sheet capacity.

## When private capacity fills

When private balance-sheet capacity is sufficient, the system absorbs flows quietly.

When it is not, liquidity seeks an absorber of last resort.

Federal Reserve facilities serve this function. Standing repo facilities, discount-window lending, and temporary programs such as the Bank Term Funding Program absorb liquidity when private intermediaries cannot or will not.

These facilities stabilize flows by preventing forced asset sales and disorderly deleveraging. They do so by temporarily holding liquidity on the central-bank balance sheet, allowing prices and balance sheets elsewhere to adjust more gradually.

They are not a permanent replacement for private intermediation. Persistent reliance would suppress price discovery, blur risk signals, and shift credit-allocation decisions from markets to policy.

## Speed and capacity

Liquidity systems are stable when the pace of reallocation is slow enough for balance sheets to adjust through pricing, funding changes, and portfolio rebalancing.

Stress emerges when liquidity moves faster than intermediaries can absorb without breaching risk limits, regulatory constraints, or operational capacity. Faster rails and better information increase efficiency in calm periods, but they compress reaction time during stress.

In this sense, modern liquidity stress is less about panic than throughput: how much liquidity can be absorbed, by whom, and how quickly.

## Why the absorber matters

Where liquidity ultimately lands determines how stress propagates.

If liquidity is absorbed by private balance sheets, adjustment occurs through pricing, portfolio shifts, and risk transfer. If it is absorbed by central-bank facilities, adjustment is delayed but stabilized: losses are not immediately realized, forced sales are avoided, and institutions are given time to restructure funding or capital positions.

If absorption capacity is unclear or contested — when private intermediaries hesitate, regulatory constraints bind, or backstops exist but are slow or stigmatized — liquidity does not stop moving. Stress migrates instead, appearing in funding markets, price dislocations, or institutional strain elsewhere in the system.

Understanding this hierarchy matters more than tracking any single instrument.
