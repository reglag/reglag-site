# Permission, With Conditions

Good morning.

**TL;DR**  
CPI left the policy floor intact, but Commerce tightened the more actionable constraint: access. BIS has created a conditional corridor for certain AI chip exports to China and Macau—revocable, capped, and documentation-heavy. Nvidia’s own filings suggest the stakes are meaningful, but access now depends less on demand than on the ability to keep proving compliance.

### CPI keeps the floor in place
The CPI print didn’t change the macro backdrop. Core inflation remains firm enough to keep policy restrictive and expectations for near-term relief muted. In practice, that leaves institutions planning as if funding costs, inventory timing, and working-capital swings must be absorbed internally rather than offset by easier conditions.

When the policy floor holds, balance sheets become less forgiving of delay.

### BIS turns Nvidia access into a corridor
The BIS rule does not reopen China chip exports outright. It replaces a presumption of denial with case-by-case review, but only so long as the exporter continuously satisfies documented conditions. For Nvidia, that means demonstrating sufficient U.S. supply, keeping China shipments within defined limits relative to domestic deliveries, submitting chips for U.S.-based third-party testing, and certifying customer identity and access controls.

If those conditions lapse—or can’t be substantiated—the review policy snaps back to denial. Access exists, but it has to be earned repeatedly.

### What’s at stake, and when
Nvidia’s own filings provide the cleanest framing without speculation. Before the most recent round of export restrictions took effect in April 2025, revenue attributed to customers headquartered in China (including Hong Kong) was running at roughly $17 billion over a nine-month period. In the most recent reported quarter after those restrictions were in place, that figure fell to about $3 billion.

The point is not the precise number. It’s that the BIS corridor is being applied to a business line that has already been materially compressed, not one that is waiting to be unlocked.

### Allocation control embedded in process
Although framed as national security, the rule functions as an allocation mechanism. By tying exports to domestic availability, shipment ratios, and testing capacity, BIS forces Nvidia to plan production and logistics around compliance thresholds rather than pure market pull.

Nothing is formally banned. It just takes longer, costs more, and can be turned off.

### Enforcement moves into the workflow
Much of the enforcement now sits at the front end. Testing, customer verification, and limits on remote access reduce reliance on penalties after the fact. BIS can revoke a testing lab’s eligibility, effectively closing the corridor without rewriting the rule.

Compliance isn’t checked once. It’s checked until further notice.

### What this adds up to
Taken together, CPI holding the policy floor and BIS tightening access through procedure point in the same direction. Capacity still exists in the system, but it is being allocated more selectively, with friction placed deliberately between capability and execution.

Activity doesn’t stop. It just starts asking for paperwork.
