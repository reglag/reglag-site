# Liquidity Reallocation in the U.S. Dollar System — Part I: Stablecoins as Narrow, Reserve-Backed Instruments
*Stablecoins as Shadow Money Market Funds*
## Weekend Deep Dive

*January 17, 2026*

Good morning.

**TL;DR**  
Dollar stablecoins increasingly function as shadow money market funds: par-redeemable liabilities backed by cash and short-dated Treasuries, operating outside the traditional bank and fund perimeter. The central question is no longer whether stablecoins work—they do—but how scale, speed, reserve mechanics, and yield equivalence interact with regulation, bank funding, and the dollar system. This piece explains what stablecoins are structurally, why that structure has proven durable, and where constraint is now forming.

This is the first of a three-part series examining stablecoins as financial infrastructure. Part I focuses on structure and mechanics. Part II examines deposit substitution and bank funding pressure. Part III considers system-level liquidity reallocation and stress transmission.

### Scope

This analysis covers fiat-backed U.S. dollar stablecoins only. Algorithmic stablecoins and non-USD tokens are out of scope. The focus is on mechanics, incentives, and institutional responses rather than advocacy.

### A brief history of discomfort

Stablecoins did not begin as a policy project. They began as a workaround.

Early crypto markets needed a dollar proxy that could move at blockchain speed without touching banks every time a trade settled. Fiat-backed stablecoins filled that gap quietly, growing alongside exchanges and trading venues rather than through retail adoption.

For several years, the regulatory response oscillated between tolerance and unease. Stablecoins were small enough to ignore, yet similar enough to deposits and money market funds to feel familiar in all the wrong ways. The tension was not novelty. It was money-like liabilities growing outside the perimeter.

That tension sharpened after repeated stress episodes: rapid expansion during risk-on periods, sharp contractions during market drawdowns, and moments where traditional banking stress spilled directly into stablecoin confidence. By the mid-2020s, it became clear that stablecoins were no longer a niche instrument but a persistent feature of dollar liquidity.

### From ambiguity to perimeter—then interpretation

The GENIUS Act marked the first serious attempt to resolve that ambiguity.

Rather than banning stablecoins or forcing them wholesale into the banking system, the law created a defined issuance perimeter. In practical terms, it made liquidity and full collateralization non-negotiable. Stablecoin issuers are required to hold high-quality liquid assets, honor par redemption, and operate under explicit governance and compliance standards.

What the Act did not provide was immediate operational closure. Implementing regulation, supervisory frameworks, and examination expectations remain in development. In practice, the binding constraints are emerging through interpretation, supervisory signaling, and enforcement posture rather than finalized rulebooks.

The perimeter now exists. The constraint surface is how it is applied.

The law does not make stablecoins safe by aspiration; it makes safety the cost of admission. Coins that cannot meet those standards simply do not qualify. That reshapes the ecosystem more durably than any single enforcement action.

### What a stablecoin actually is

At its core, a fiat-backed stablecoin is a simple promise wrapped in new plumbing.

Dollars go in. Tokens are issued. Tokens return. Dollars are redeemed.

The critical element is the reserve pool in between. That pool is not an implementation detail; it is the product. When reserves consist primarily of cash, Treasury bills, and overnight repo, the stablecoin behaves less like a speculative crypto asset and more like a tokenized pool of government money.

Large dollar stablecoins such as USDT and USDC operate this way in practice, allowing dollar-denominated value to circulate on digital rails backed by conventional financial assets.

The wrapper is novel. The balance sheet is not.

This is why stablecoins consistently trigger debates that feel oddly familiar. They look new, but they behave like instruments policymakers have argued about for decades.

### A narrow-bank analogy, with limits

The most useful analogy is not a checking account. It is narrow banking or government money market funds.

Stablecoins promise par redemption and hold high-quality liquid assets. They do not transform maturity or credit. By contrast, bank deposits are claims on leveraged balance sheets that transform both maturity and credit—an arrangement that is economically productive, but structurally fragile under stress.

That narrowness is precisely what gives stablecoins stability in normal conditions.

Where the analogy breaks is speed. Stablecoins settle continuously, across borders, without market hours or clearing windows. In calm periods, this is an efficiency gain. In stress, speed becomes a variable.

Money market funds learned this lesson slowly, with committees and comment letters. Stablecoins will learn it in milliseconds.

### The absence of stated interest—and the yield equivalence problem

Stablecoins are often described as non-interest-bearing. A more accurate description is the absence of stated interest.

Economically, reserves earn yield. The question is who receives it, and how transparently.

USDC itself does not pay interest to holders. However, platforms that custody USDC may offer rewards economically linked to the yield earned on reserves. The return is not labeled interest on the stablecoin; it is framed as a platform benefit.

This distinction is not cosmetic. It reveals where the regulatory boundary actually sits.

That boundary is now under active pressure. Current policy debates, including those reflected in the CLARITY Act draft, are centered on preventing stablecoins from becoming de facto savings instruments even as reserve economics make that outcome difficult to avoid. Industry responses have been pragmatic rather than ideological: yield is delivered through wrappers, platforms, and incentives that preserve formal distinctions while testing how far economic equivalence can travel without triggering regulatory equivalence.

If that boundary is ever resolved—explicitly or implicitly—stablecoins begin to resemble superior savings instruments to traditional bank deposits from a user perspective: liquid, portable, transparent, and tied to risk-free rates rather than retail banking spreads. That prospect explains why this debate has become persistent rather than episodic.

### A familiar shadow: the Eurodollar echo

There is a historical rhyme worth noting.

A Eurodollar is a U.S. dollar deposit held outside the United States, typically at a foreign bank, and therefore outside direct Federal Reserve supervision. Eurodollars expanded global dollar usage without passing through the domestic regulatory perimeter and became central to international trade and finance.

Stablecoins are not Eurodollars. Eurodollars are bank liabilities, often fractional and credit-based. Stablecoins are fully reserved and digitally native. But the structural similarity is instructive: both extend dollar usage beyond the domestic banking system.

In both cases, growth was driven by utility first and regulation later. Supervision tightened only after scale and stress made the perimeter unavoidable.

History does not repeat, but it does occasionally clear its throat.

### Why stablecoins persist

Stablecoins persist because they solve real frictions.

They move dollars across borders without correspondent banks. They settle on holidays. They reduce counterparty risk for exchange settlement and collateral management. They provide dollar access where local banking is unreliable or constrained.

What has changed is the marginal user. Stablecoins are no longer only crypto-market plumbing. They are increasingly tolerated—and selectively adopted—inside institutions as efficiency layers for custody, collateral movement, and internal settlement. That shift increases regulatory and systemic salience even if headline volumes do not explode.

From a user perspective, stablecoins can also look like better stores of value than bank deposits, even without stated interest. Balances are transparent, settlement is instant, and exposure is to government collateral rather than a leveraged bank balance sheet. The trade-off has been yield labeling, not economic substance.

### Extending the dollar, complicating the map

Stablecoins extend the functional reach of the U.S. dollar.

They allow dollar-denominated value to circulate globally through software rather than branches. This can reinforce dollar usage even as it complicates visibility and control.

From an institutional perspective, this creates ambivalence. Broader dollar usage is welcome. Dollar movement through lightly supervised rails is less so. That tension explains why sanctions enforcement, address-blocking authority, and off-ramp governance loom so large in stablecoin policy.

Stablecoins do not challenge the dollar. They change how it travels.

### Scale and concentration context

Scale matters, but context matters more.

The largest U.S. government money market funds each hold several hundred billion dollars in assets—an order of magnitude larger than the largest GENIUS-compliant dollar stablecoins today. Larger offshore or non-aligned issuers exist, but they sit outside the regulatory and supervisory perimeter examined here.

Stablecoins remain smaller in absolute terms, but they are more concentrated, operate continuously, and settle at machine speed. If systemic relevance emerges, it is unlikely to come from size alone. It would arise from coordination, velocity, and reserve behavior under stress rather than from headline balances.

### The Treasury demand channel

When stablecoin reserves are invested in Treasury bills and repo, growth creates incremental demand for short-dated U.S. government debt.

This does not fund deficits. It marginally absorbs issuance at the short end. In a high-issuance environment, marginal buyers matter.

At sufficient scale, reserve behavior is no longer purely passive. Under stress, reserve managers may become procyclical sellers rather than steady absorbers, interacting with bill issuance concentration and repo capacity. Whether reserve demand dampens or transmits volatility depends on speed, coordination, and liquidity composition.

### Charters as stratification, not ignition

Federal bank charters matter for stablecoins, but as a stratification mechanism rather than a growth catalyst.

Several crypto and stablecoin firms have pursued or discussed national trust bank charters or similar structures. These moves strengthen reserve credibility under federal supervision and reduce survivability risk.

What charters do not provide is direct access to Federal Reserve settlement, liquidity facilities, or the discount window. No stablecoin issuer currently holds a Federal Reserve master account. That boundary—not the charter itself—remains the true dividing line.

Stablecoins are not being regulated into existence. They are being regulated into stratification.

### Run mechanics, step by step

If confidence in a stablecoin falters, the mechanics are straightforward.

First, holders seek redemption. Second, issuers meet those redemptions using cash or by liquidating reserve assets. Third, if redemptions accelerate, Treasury bills may be sold or repo positions unwound.

In practice, operational frictions often surface before balance-sheet limits. Redemption queues, banking-hour mismatches, and settlement bottlenecks between token rails and fiat rails can become binding even when reserves are ample.

At small scale, this is routine liquidity management. At large scale, it becomes market-relevant. This is why regulators focus on liquidity composition, redemption timing, and operational resilience rather than on headline market capitalization.

### What to watch

Several indicators will matter more than commentary.

Watch scale: growth rates, issuer concentration, and reserve size.  
Watch speed: redemption velocity, settlement bottlenecks, and on-chain versus off-chain flows.  
Watch substitution: evidence of stablecoins displacing bank deposits or money fund balances.  
Watch stratification: which issuers gain regulatory privileges or infrastructure access over time.

This piece explains the instrument. The next pieces examine where pressure appears and how the system absorbs it.
