# When Policy Isn’t Law Yet — But Behavior Has Moved

Good morning.

**TL;DR:**  
Today’s constraint signals share a common origin and a common feature. They are being driven from the White House, and none are law, rule, or even draft regulation yet. Section 232 has been invoked to place critical minerals into a security review channel, affordability pressure is reshaping consumer credit through posture rather than statute, and Fed independence is being tested via oversight rather than mandate. All are incomplete — and already changing behavior.

### Section 232: authority invoked, dollars at stake
The White House has formally invoked Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, which authorizes the President to restrict imports deemed to threaten national security after a Commerce Department review. That matters because Section 232 is statutory authority, not a policy memo — but it is also not a tariff schedule yet.

To ground the scale: the U.S. imports tens of billions of dollars’ worth of critical minerals and processed derivatives each year, with rare earth elements, permanent magnets, and downstream components concentrated in a small number of foreign supply chains. Rare earth magnets sit at the center of defense systems, EV motors, wind turbines, and data-center infrastructure.

What exists today is a review and negotiation mandate, not a numeric measure. Commerce is directed to assess supply chains and negotiate outcomes, with discretion over timing, scope, and remedies. That uncertainty is the constraint. Once an input enters a Section 232 channel, firms plan around sequencing risk rather than price — contracts shorten, buffers widen, and “politically uncomplicated” supply carries a premium.

Historically, Section 232 rarely produces a clean endpoint; its primary effect is to keep inputs in a negotiable, revocable channel where behavior adjusts long before policy settles.

This is industrial policy by corridor. The numbers come later.

### Credit-card affordability: posture without a rule
The push to cap credit-card rates at 10% is also White House-driven, but even earlier-stage. There is no statute, no draft regulation, and no agency formally claiming implementation authority.

What exists is public pressure, framed as affordability. That has been enough to prompt warnings from banks and at least one voluntary pricing experiment. This is not compliance; it is anticipation.

At this stage, the regulatory status is best described as pre-legislative, pre-regulatory signaling. But the behavioral response is already real. Institutions are modeling credit rationing, fee substitution, and underwriting changes — because if discretion hardens later, being unprepared is more expensive than adjusting early.

If a cap ever becomes binding, the system won’t absorb it quietly. It will ration.

### Fed independence: oversight without mandate change
Scrutiny of the Federal Reserve follows the same pattern. There is no change to the Fed’s mandate, no draft legislation, and no rulemaking. What exists instead is oversight and investigatory pressure, applied through legal process and public signaling.

Markets can price restrictive policy. What they struggle to hedge is uncertainty about whether institutional independence is being contested through process rather than law. At this stage, it is incomplete regulation — but complete enough to introduce credibility volatility.

This is where independence risk shows up first: not in rates, but in posture.

### What this adds up to
Across all three cases, authority is being asserted early, discretion is emphasized, and final form is deferred. None of these are finished rules. That is precisely why they matter.

Constraint formation has moved upstream. Behavior is adjusting before law arrives. The rulebook is still blank, but everyone is already reading between the lines.

### On deck (next 7–10 days)
We should expect further detail on how Commerce intends to operationalize the Section 232 review for critical minerals, including whether interim measures apply during negotiations. Credit issuers and banks are likely to continue signaling how they would reprice and ration if affordability pressure advances into legislation. The next H.4.1 release from the Federal Reserve will provide a check on reserve conditions as institutional pressures persist.

### Regulatory radar (next 30–90 days)
Section 232 is likely to remain a sequencing tool rather than a one-time action, particularly for inputs tied to defense, energy, and data-center infrastructure. Affordability pressure may migrate from rhetoric into narrower legislative or supervisory instruments, with carve-outs doing most of the work. Oversight-driven process risk at the Fed is likely to remain elevated absent a clear boundary reset.

No trades. Today’s developments reinforce existing constraints rather than introduce a new one that would justify repositioning.
