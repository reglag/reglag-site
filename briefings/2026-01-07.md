## OFAC is not easing Russia sanctions. It is tightening the definition of “ordinary business.”

OFAC’s Russia-related General License 13P does one thing, and it does it with the kind of specificity that makes compliance teams feel both relieved and vaguely insulted. It authorizes U.S. persons to pay taxes, fees, import duties, and to purchase or receive permits, licenses, registrations, certifications, and even tax refunds, but only to the extent those transactions would otherwise be prohibited by Directive 4 under Executive Order 14024. It also comes with an expiration date, and it replaces the prior version of the license.

Read the structure and you see the intent.

Directive 4 is where the U.S. told the world that the Central Bank of Russia, the National Wealth Fund, and the Ministry of Finance are not normal counterparties. The license exists because “don’t transact with them” collides with the reality that businesses still face routine administrative obligations in Russia, and those obligations often route through exactly those institutions. License 13P is OFAC acknowledging that reality while trying to prevent it from becoming a loophole.

The carve-outs matter as much as the authorization. 13P does not authorize debits to accounts on the books of U.S. financial institutions for those Russian institutions, and it does not authorize dealings with blocked persons unless separately authorized. That is a polite way of saying there is no “we were just paying a fee” defense if the payment path is sloppy.

This is a corridor, not a doorway. OFAC is signaling that it wants day-to-day operations to remain legible and auditable, but it wants anything ambiguous to be slow, documented, and subject to discretion. That changes behavior even without changing the underlying sanctions perimeter.

The practical change you will see inside institutions is escalation. Payments that were treated as operational will be treated as sanctions events, because the most expensive mistakes in this category are rarely willful. They are “ordinarily incident and necessary” in a memo and “why did you do this” in an exam.

Operationally, administrative payments are increasingly being treated as guilty until proven boring. In practice, payments that can be explained in one sentence tend to move quietly, while those that require two sentences and a diagram tend to acquire a file, a meeting, and eventually a question about licensing.

## Venezuela is still a plumbing story. Today it gained a price tag.

Yesterday we covered the shipping data that mattered, including Reuters reporting tankers leaving Venezuelan waters without flags or safety documents. That was the sanctions story doing what sanctions increasingly do. It does not always stop flows. It raises the cost of making them look legitimate.

Overnight, Reuters reported a U.S.–Venezuela arrangement that would allow Venezuela to export up to $2 billion worth of crude oil to the United States, with proceeds controlled by President Trump and oversight described as falling under the Energy Secretary. If accurate and implemented, this is a material development because it formalizes a channel rather than simply squeezing informal ones.

The market implication is not simply “more Venezuelan oil.” The implication is that sanctions enforcement is moving into a phase where the U.S. tries to convert leverage into controlled flows, with the compliance perimeter doing the actual work.

In markets, controlled flows tend to dampen spot volatility while raising event risk. The oil market can remain well supplied in the near term and still price a wider range of outcomes around enforcement and logistics. That is the difference between “barrels” and “permission.”

For some, this is where ambiguity becomes expensive. If the U.S. is issuing authorizations or licenses that route Venezuelan crude to the U.S., the primary questions become very specific and very operational.

Who is the authorized seller. Who is the authorized buyer. What vessels are allowed. Which insurers and banks are comfortable touching the trade. What representations are required. What happens if counterparties change mid-voyage, or if a vessel’s documentation is questioned after it leaves port.

In regimes like this, trouble tends to start when controlled transactional programs are treated like geopolitical headlines rather than paperwork-intensive systems with long memories.

When sanctions become operational, the winners are rarely the bold. They are the boring, the ones who can reconcile a bill of lading, a payment instruction, and a beneficial ownership record without needing a miracle.

## Stablecoins, briefly

Yesterday’s stablecoin section was about deposit competition disguised as innovation. There is no new legislative text overnight that changes the analysis, and that is fine. These fights move slowly until they move all at once. The signal to watch is language convergence, especially around what counts as yield, rewards, or economically equivalent inducements. Once lawmakers start defining the perimeter, business models tend to contort before they comply, and exam questions arrive before final rules.

## Regulatory radar for the next 30–90 days

High probability  
OFAC continues to tighten through facilitation, intermediaries, and licensing calibration rather than big new program announcements. The leading indicator is the issuance of narrow authorizations like GL 13P, paired with FAQ refreshes and targeted enforcement actions.

Medium probability  
A Venezuela sanctions regime that becomes increasingly license-driven for specific flows, with more explicit carve-outs around who can trade and how proceeds are controlled. The leading indicator is any additional guidance on authorized buyers, ports, or shipping requirements.

Medium probability  
Stablecoin regulation advances through definitional tightening around inducements and affiliate structures rather than sweeping bans. The leading indicator is language convergence in committee hearings and revised draft text that targets indirect yield.
