### Venezuela is no longer a country story. It is a maritime stack story.

Reuters reported that several tankers carrying Venezuelan crude have been leaving without proper flag registration or safety documentation, and that a broader flotilla has moved in “dark mode” despite the U.S. blockade announced in mid-December. That is not background color. It is the enforcement surface.

International maritime law and the insurance ecosystem are built on boring things like flags and classification. When those disappear, you don’t just lose visibility. You lose ports, coverage, financing, and the willingness of respectable intermediaries to touch the trade. Sanctions then stop being a list and start behaving like a system, because the system has multiple chokepoints that can be tightened without ever issuing a new press release.

Treasury signaled exactly that posture last week. In its public statement, OFAC sanctioned four companies operating in Venezuela’s oil sector and identified four associated tankers as blocked property. You can treat that as “another designation,” or you can treat it as an architectural note. The target is not just the regime. The target is the commercial wiring that turns crude into usable revenue.

This is why markets are reacting the way they are. It is not an oil-shortage day. It is a permissions-risk day. Gold is sharply higher this morning, long-duration Treasuries are modestly firmer, and equities are not panicking. The tape is doing what it does when it senses friction rather than famine.

### The stablecoin fight is really a deposits fight

The American Bankers Association’s community bankers are urging Congress to close what they call a loophole in the GENIUS Act, where stablecoin issuers are barred from paying interest but affiliates may try to offer rewards that look suspiciously similar. The banking industry’s argument is straightforward. If customers can “park” money in a stablecoin and earn inducements, some deposits migrate out of banks, and community lending capacity shrinks.

The second-order effect for markets is also straightforward. Once Congress starts legislating the difference between “yield” and “rewards,” you are no longer in a product debate. You are in a funding debate. That debate tends to end the same way most funding debates end, with someone discovering that words like “economically equivalent” have sharp edges.

### CFPB and global tax show the same theme from different angles

A federal judge has ruled that the CFPB must continue to seek and receive funding through its established mechanism rather than being quietly starved. That matters because a living CFPB means consumer finance remains a live regulatory front in 2026, even if other parts of Washington are signaling a lighter touch. It also matters because the “death by funding” strategy is now less credible, which changes how industry should handicap the next year of rulemaking and enforcement.

Separately, Treasury announced an agreement stating that OECD Pillar Two will not apply to U.S.-headquartered multinationals under the revised global minimum tax framework, and Reuters reported that more than 145 countries agreed updates to the global minimum tax deal that address U.S. concerns. That is good news for U.S. multinationals in the short run, and a potential source of diplomatic and policy friction in the medium run. International tax coordination does not usually fail with fireworks. It fails by exemptions, then carve-outs, then footnotes, then everyone pretending the footnotes were always the plan.

### What is on deck in the next 7–10 days

This is a week where the calendar can manufacture volatility without any help from geopolitics.

The SEC has a closed meeting scheduled for Thursday, Jan 8, per its Sunshine Act notice. Closed meetings are not always consequential, but they often correlate with enforcement cadence and settlement mechanics. Treasury’s tentative auction schedule shows a full coupon cycle next week, including 3-year and 10-year note auctions on Monday, Jan 12 and a 30-year bond reopening on Tuesday, Jan 13, with additional bill supply layered around it. The BLS schedule has the jobs report on Friday, Jan 9 and CPI on Tuesday, Jan 13. That is a very efficient way to test the rates market’s confidence.

My expectation is that volatility risk rises into that cluster. The base case is not “crisis.” The base case is “larger intraday ranges and more conditional liquidity,” which is how desks discover which assumptions were too comfortable.

One more observation, because it will come up in your inbox. When counterparties ask for a “quick refresh” of sanctions representations, it is rarely because they are feeling relaxed.
